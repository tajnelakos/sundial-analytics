Key findings

The winning subject-line style flipped between the two personas — same technique, opposite result
For Head of Credit Risk, Variant B (naming their specific fear as a direct question — "What your auditor will actually ask") beat Variant A (stating the same fact declaratively) on every metric: open rate 56.6% vs 50.5%, reply rate 5.1% vs 2.8%, meetings booked 14 vs 8. For VP of Mortgage Lending, it's the mirror image: Variant A (a concrete number, stated directly — "The cycle-time number lenders like you actually hit") beat Variant B (the question framing) on every metric: open rate 60.3% vs 50.1%, reply rate 5.8% vs 3.1%, meetings booked 19 vs 9. The lesson isn't "questions win" or "declarative wins" — it's that specificity wins, and what counts as specific differs by persona: for a skeptical risk buyer, specificity means naming their actual fear precisely; for a speed-and-numbers buyer, specificity means the number itself.
Reply and meeting rates tick up at Email 4 in both sequences — the email built around each persona's own named trust trigger
Both sequences show a steady decline in engagement from Email 1 through Email 3, then a reply/meeting uptick at Email 4 before resuming the decline at Email 5. Email 4 is not a repeat of an earlier angle in either sequence — for Head of Credit Risk it's the peer-reference email (personas.md names "peer references from similarly-regulated institutions" as a specific trust trigger for this persona); for VP of Mortgage Lending it's the real week-by-week rollout timeline (personas.md names "a rep who separates 'the tool goes live' from 'my team actually adopts it'" as this persona's trust trigger). Replies at Email 4: HoCR 6 (vs. 4 at Email 3), VP 8 (vs. 5 at Email 3). This reads as evidence that the trust-trigger mapping in the sequence design is doing real work, not just decoration.
VP of Mortgage Lending converts at a higher overall rate, consistent with the persona's psychology, not just better copy
VP's 2.30% meeting rate against HoCR's 1.92% tracks with what personas.md already says about the two: Head of Credit Risk is risk-averse by function and has "usually been burned once already" by explainability claims, so this persona's skepticism is expected to suppress conversion somewhat regardless of copy quality — the sequence's job is to be one of the few that survives that skepticism, not to eliminate it.
Neither sequence's final email spiked unsubscribes — the no-urgency close appears not to cost reach
Email 5 in both campaigns carries zero unsubscribes across both variants, despite being the sequence-ending email. Combined with branding-guideline's ban on manufactured urgency, this is a small piece of evidence that ending on a genuine, low-pressure offer (a live demo on the buyer's own data; a joint walkthrough with their risk team) doesn't cost list health the way a "last chance" close might have.

What this means for the next sequence

Keep persona-specific subject-line testing — don't standardize on one winning style repo-wide
Applying VP's winning declarative style to the Head of Credit Risk campaign, or vice versa, would likely have cost both campaigns performance based on this data. Any future persona campaign (Broker Team Lead, Credit Risk Analyst / Data Lead) should run its own A/B test rather than inherit either winner by default.
Consider moving the Email 4 trust-trigger content earlier
If the trust-trigger email is reliably the one that reverses engagement decay, testing it at Email 2 or 3 instead of Email 4 in the next iteration of either sequence would show whether the lift is about content (moves with the email) or position (a natural rebound partway through any sequence) — this data set can't distinguish the two on its own.
